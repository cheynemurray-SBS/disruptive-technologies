Disruptive Technologies

Why the most dangerous competitor is rarely the one building a better version of what you already sell.

Core Concept Sustaining vs. Disruptive Technologies

Every technology follows some trajectory of improvement over time. The critical managerial skill isn't spotting new technology — it's telling the difference between a technology that will sustain your current competitive position and one that will disrupt it.

Sustaining Technology

A technology that maintains or rejuvenates the current rate of performance improvement of the products and services that use it. It offers the same set of attributes customers already value, just delivered with greater performance, in direct response to market demand.

Disruptive Technology

A technology with different performance attributes than the one currently in use, whose rate of improvement outpaces what the market actually demands — eventually overtaking the incumbent approach entirely.

Definitions adapted from Piccoli, G., & Pigni, F. (2021). Information Systems for Managers with Cases.

Sustaining innovation is what most R&D budgets are built to produce: faster processors, longer battery life, sharper displays — improvements along a trajectory customers already understand and are willing to pay for. Disruptive innovation is different in kind, not just degree. It often starts by performing worse on the metrics that matter to a firm's best customers today, which is exactly why incumbents tend to dismiss it — right up until it doesn't.

Core Concept The Two Defining Characteristics

Per Piccoli and Pigni (2021), a technology qualifies as disruptive when it exhibits both of the following characteristics — either one alone is not enough:

1

Different Characteristics

It offers a different set of attributes than the technology currently in use at the firm — not simply a faster or cheaper version of the same thing, but a genuinely different value proposition (often smaller, simpler, cheaper, or more convenient, even if less powerful at first).

2

Performance Improvement Rate Exceeds Market Demand

Its rate of performance improvement is greater than the rate the market actually demands. Because of this, it eventually "overshoots" what customers need on the old metric, while also delivering something new they hadn't been asking for.

Why Successful Firms Miss It

Incumbents usually aren't blindsided by disruptive technology because they failed to notice it — they often see it early. The trap is that listening closely to their best, most profitable customers (good management practice, by every conventional measure) leads them to systematically underinvest in a technology those same customers don't yet want.

By the time the disruptive technology's performance crosses the market-demand line, the incumbent's resource allocation process has already starved the new approach of investment for years — and a smaller, more nimble entrant owns the emerging market instead.

Not yet saved

A useful example from higher education itself is the rise of asynchronous online course delivery. Early online courses offered a different attribute set than the in-person lecture: lower cost, greater flexibility, and accessibility for working adults — but at first, weaker production quality, less real-time interaction, and less prestige than a traditional classroom.

Over time, the tools supporting online delivery (video, interactive modules, learning analytics, adaptive assessment) improved at a rate that outpaced what most students actually needed to earn a rigorous credential. What began as a lower-quality alternative for a narrow niche — working professionals who couldn't attend in person — became a legitimate, sometimes preferred, delivery mode for a much broader population, challenging institutions that built their entire model around in-person instruction.

Not yet saved

Well-run organizations typically build their resource allocation process around listening to their most profitable, most vocal current customers, and funding the projects with the clearest near-term return. Disruptive technologies almost never look attractive by those criteria at first — they usually serve a smaller, lower-margin segment, and underperform on the metrics current customers care about most.

That means the very discipline that makes an organization excellent at serving its existing market — rigorous financial hurdles, deep customer listening, efficient resource allocation — is often what causes it to systematically defund or ignore the disruptive alternative until it's too late to catch up. The organizational strength becomes a blind spot.

Not yet saved

Rather than evaluating the disruptive opportunity with the same financial hurdles and market-sizing logic used for sustaining projects, many executives create a separate, smaller unit — often deliberately insulated from the core business's customers, margins, and reporting structure — to pursue it. This lets the new initiative be judged against the size and needs of the emerging market it's actually trying to serve, rather than being killed for looking too small relative to the core business.

Just as importantly, this means being willing to fund a technology that current best customers say they don't want, and tracking its progress against the emerging market's trajectory rather than the existing market's demand curve.
